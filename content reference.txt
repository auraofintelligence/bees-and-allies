You’re right. The plan does not need to sound like Straddie beekeepers are passive observers waiting for mainland experts. The revised premise is:

**The bees are already under pressure on Minjerribah / North Stradbroke Island; local beekeepers are already acting; the lab could add new capacity for Straddie; universities could be invited in as partners to support local leadership, not replace it.**

The earlier advice can be reframed from “don’t bring pests to the island” to **“do not import or casually move pests, but if mites and beetles are already being found by local beekeepers, build an island verification, response, research and training lab around that reality.”** Queensland already treats *Varroa destructor* as notifiable restricted matter, and official Queensland guidance says beekeepers must report, control or contain it; it also notes resistant varroa has been detected in South East Queensland. ([Business Queensland][1]) Small hive beetle is also highly plausible locally because Bee Aware says it has had major impact through the warm, humid coastal strip from Victoria to North Queensland. ([beeaware.org.au][2])

## Revised plan: Straddie Bee Resilience Lab

Call it something like:

**Minjerribah / Straddie Bee Resilience Lab**
A locally led, university-backed island lab for bee pest surveillance, practical control, native-plant bioassays, beekeeper training, and community biosecurity.

The mission can be blunt and practical:

**Keep Straddie’s bees alive, support self-sovereign beekeepers, generate publishable science, and turn the island into a model for community-led bee biosecurity.**

The corrected premise is not to move into or lean on an existing facility as the assumed base. The project can add capacity rather than take capacity from marine research, community facilities or any other island work. The first phase could be a **digital-twin-first planning lab**: model site options, access, neighbours, services, Country, transport, risk, mobile routes and new-build needs before choosing any physical location. The eventual island lab can still be real, but the site can be selected through co-op stewardship and evidence rather than declared up front.

## Core change in strategy

The local beekeeper reports become the beginning of the project, not an anecdote on the side.

The first lab question is not “could pests arrive?” It is:

**What exactly is already on the island, where is it, how heavy is the pressure, what are beekeepers already doing, and what support would actually help?**

That means the first work package is an **Island Bee Health Census**, run with local beekeepers.

It could include varroa monitoring, small hive beetle counts, hive strength, colony losses, treatments already used, known slime-outs, queen status, brood pattern, honey flow conditions, native forage conditions, and whether the same yards are seeing both mite and beetle pressure. The uploaded report noted that beetle losses can become worse where colonies are already stressed by mites, which is exactly why Straddie needs a combined mite-and-beetle plan rather than a single-pest response.

## The actual lab on Straddie

The fastest real-world version is a **digital-twin-first new-capacity model**.

**Layer 1: digital twin and site-options model.**
No location is chosen. The first working model could map possible new sites, mobile lab routes, service needs, access, neighbours, roads, water, power, waste, cultural respect, fire risk, field proximity, training space and community benefit. It could explicitly ask: what would add bee-health capacity without taking away from existing research, public or community facilities?

**Layer 2: community apiary and beekeeper field nodes.**
These could be locally agreed demonstration and sentinel apiary options, also tested in the digital twin before any physical commitment. Possible nodes could be near different communities or mobile rather than fixed, depending on land access, neighbours, fire risk, vehicle access, cultural respect and beekeeper preference. This is where local beekeepers test practical monitoring systems, trap designs, hive hygiene methods, queen/brood-break techniques, and registered treatments under proper guidance.

The lab could have six rooms, benches or zones:

| Zone                         | Purpose                                                                    | Minimum equipment                                                                               |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Sample receiving bench       | Log samples from local hives without cross-contamination                   | sealed sample boxes, labels, chain-of-custody forms, freezer, disinfectant station              |
| Microscopy and ID bench      | Confirm mites, beetles, larvae, brood symptoms                             | stereomicroscopes, compound microscope, camera mount, reference ID sheets                       |
| Bee safety and bioassay room | Test candidate plant extracts and trap materials safely before hive trials | bee cages, incubator, humidity control, scales, microplates, timer/data logging                 |
| Small hive beetle room       | Controlled work on beetle behaviour, traps, repellents and attractants     | double-screened insectary cabinet, trap prototypes, soil pupation containers, freezer kill step |
| Training and data room       | Beekeeper workshops, dashboard, reporting help                             | screen, laptop station, printed protocols, map wall, secure beekeeper data system               |

High-end GC-MS chemistry, qPCR confirmation, residues and viral diagnostics can be outsourced at first to universities, CSIRO or another partner. The Straddie lab still remains real because the **sampling, microscopy, field trials, training, beetle work, beekeeper support, digital twin and island dataset** happen for the island, with new capacity added over time.

## First 30 days: local action, not paperwork paralysis

The first move is to form a **Straddie Bee Pest Working Group**.

It could include:

Local beekeepers from Amity, Dunwich and Point Lookout; Traditional Custodians and community representatives who choose to join; university and technical partners; Biosecurity Queensland / DPI; Queensland Beekeepers’ Association; Southern Cross University’s Bee Research and Extension Lab; Redland City Council; digital-twin/site-options helpers; and one person responsible for chosen sharing levels so beekeepers can contribute on their own terms.

The first 30 days could produce four things.

**1. A beekeeper-owned working register for the project.**
Not a forced public map of everyone’s hives. A working register with opt-in sharing levels, from broad island patterns through to named examples where people choose that.

**2. A local pest evidence protocol.**
Every suspicious mite or beetle sample gets photographed, labelled by date and hive code, checked under a microscope, and preserved. Varroa detections go through official channels because Queensland requires reporting and control/containment. ([Business Queensland][1]) Queensland’s Bee 123 pathway is already used for varroa hive checks and reporting. ([Business Queensland][5])

**3. A “beekeeper first aid” kit.**
Alcohol wash/sugar shake kits, sticky boards or equivalent monitoring tools, small hive beetle traps, sample jars, gloves, hive record sheets, QR-coded reporting help, and clear instructions on what not to move between apiaries.

**4. A one-page Straddie Bee Health Situation Report.**
This is the document used to approach universities and funders. It says: local beekeepers are observing mites and beetles; Queensland has an active varroa management problem; Straddie has an existing research station; the community is ready; and partners are invited to help build an island lab.

## First 90 days: prove the need and make universities useful

The 90-day pilot could be called:

**Island Bee Health Census and Rapid Response Pilot**

It could do the following:

| ------------------------- | --------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Local hive census         | Local beekeepers + lab technician | number of participating hives, de-identified zones, hive strength baseline |
| Pest confirmation         | Straddie lab + Biosecurity Qld    | confirmed ID records for varroa, SHB, suspicious exotic pests              |

| Funding proposal          | working group                     | grant-ready case for a permanent lab                                       |

This pilot does not require the island to wait for a PhD project. The science begins with beekeeper observations, then adds university-grade measurement.

## How to get local universities involved

Do not send universities a vague “please help the bees” request. Send them a **structured partner pack** with a clear ask.

The partner pack could include:

1. **Problem:** mites and beetles are being reported by Straddie beekeepers; Queensland has entered management mode; SEQ has resistant varroa concerns. ([Business Queensland][1])
2. **Asset:** Straddie has local beekeepers, field reality, community energy and a chance to add new bee-health capacity rather than occupying someone else’s facility.
3. **Community:** local beekeepers are organised and willing to provide samples, field observations and practical knowledge.
4. **Stewardship:** the project could invite everyone into a co-op model of responsible stewardship. No organisation is assumed to be in charge before the conversation happens.
5. **Research value:** bee biosecurity, resistant varroa, small hive beetle, native plant chemistry, community surveillance and island ecology.
6. **Ask:** nominate one academic lead, one technical contact, one student project pathway, and one in-kind facility/service contribution.

### Best partner sequence

**1. Digital twin and site-options partners**
Ask universities, council, local mappers, civic technologists, makers and beekeepers to build a digital twin before choosing any location. The first partner ask is modelling, not occupation of an existing facility.

**2. Southern Cross University Bee Research and Extension Lab**
Ask SCU to lead practical bee-health extension and IPM design. SCU’s Bee Research and Extension Lab says its focus includes honey bee pests, diseases and biosecurity, affordable and effective IPM strategies, and Varroa and Tropilaelaps mites. ([Southern Cross University][8])

**3. UQ / University of the Sunshine Coast linked varroa miticide research**
There is already a project listed through UQ Experts on precision receptor-specific miticides, aiming to characterise varroa receptors and develop varroa-specific miticides safe for bees. That is exactly the kind of high-end science the Straddie lab could feed with field samples and practical beekeeper data. ([About][9])

**4. QUT / Griffith / UniSQ / JCU as specialist partners**
Use them for sensors, dashboards, environmental science, social licence, chemistry, education design, agriculture extension and tropical biosecurity. They do not all need to own the project. They can own specific work packages.

**5. DPI, QBA, Plant Health Australia and CSIRO as official/technical advisors**
Keep them close so the lab does not accidentally become a parallel rogue biosecurity system. That matters because varroa is notifiable and movement/treatment rules can change.

The lab could have a founding co-op stewardship structure like this:

| Seat                                        | Role                                                      |
| ------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------- |
| 2 local beekeeper seats                     | practical hive knowledge, field priorities, early warning |
| Country and community care seats            | cultural respect, place, benefit-sharing, local jobs       |

| 1 Redland / local economic development seat | facilities, grants, community connection                  |
| 1 independent science chair                 | keeps research standards high                             |
| 1 sharing steward                           | helps beekeepers choose their contribution level           |

The key principle: **beekeepers are not “participants”; they are co-researchers.**

They could be paid for structured data collection when funding allows. Local knowledge can be acknowledged in reports. No student or academic gets to extract data, publish and vanish. No worries, mate — that sort of academic fly-in/fly-out nonsense would get swatted faster than a hive beetle on the lid.

## Biosecurity and legal pathway

This plan can be bold without being reckless.

The operating rules could be:

**Do not import mites or beetles to Straddie.** Work with what is already present, lawfully collected, or officially supplied under permit.

**Report notifiable pests.** Queensland says varroa is category 2 restricted matter and remains notifiable; beekeepers must report, control or contain it. ([Business Queensland][1])

**Register and support local hives.** Queensland requires registration as a biosecurity entity if someone keeps one or more European honey bee hives. ([Business Queensland][10])

**Follow the general biosecurity obligation.** Queensland’s GBO requires people and corporations to manage biosecurity risks under their control and not spread pests, diseases or contaminants. ([Qld Primary Industries][11])

**Use APVMA pathways for experimental treatments.** If the lab wants to trial unregistered or off-label agvet chemicals, including plant-based products used as pest controls in hives, APVMA research permit rules matter. APVMA says research permits legalise research use of agvet chemicals in Australia, and some small-scale trials may fit within PER7250 if all conditions are met. ([APVMA][12])

**Handle native plant work properly.** Queensland biodiscovery rules require collection authority and benefit-sharing arrangements before collecting and using native biological resources from State land or Queensland waters for biodiscovery. ([Environment Queensland][13]) A benefit-sharing agreement must be in place before biodiscovery begins. ([Business Queensland][14])

## Research program: what the Straddie lab could actually do

The uploaded report already identified eucalypt chemistry, lemon myrtle and tea tree as priority native-plant candidates, while warning that bee safety, residues and delivery systems matter more than whether something kills pests in a dish.  The Straddie lab could turn that into field-relevant work.

### Workstream A: pest confirmation and surveillance

Monthly local monitoring:

| Target            | Method                                                                                     |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Varroa            | alcohol wash/sugar shake, brood inspection, sticky boards, photo ID, optional eDNA samples |
| Small hive beetle | trap counts, adult beetle counts, larvae/slime-out records, soil/pupation risk notes       |
| Brood diseases    | brood pattern records, dead brood photos, lab referral if suspicious                       |
| Colony stress     | queen status, hive strength, nutrition, beetle correlation, recent treatment               |
| Native bees       | non-invasive observations only, with separate protocols from honey bee hives               |

### Workstream B: local beekeeper response

This is where the lab earns trust.

The lab could run monthly practical sessions:

**“Bring your board, beetles and brood frame photos.”**
Beekeepers bring samples or images. The lab helps identify the problem and records anonymised data.

**“Treatment effectiveness checks.”**
Not “spray this magic oil.” Instead: monitor before treatment, use legal treatments properly, monitor after treatment, record whether mite counts actually fell.

**“Small hive beetle pressure days.”**
Trap design, hive hygiene, avoiding deadouts and slime-outs, reducing empty space, managing weak colonies, and sharing what is working locally.

**“Queen and brood-break methods.”**
Teach brood interruption as a serious management tool, but with care around beetle risk because queen cages and weak colonies can create small hive beetle opportunities if done badly.

### Workstream C: native plant bioassays

Start with lab screens, not field hype.

Candidate groups:

| Candidate                              | Why                                                                        |
| -------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Eucalypt oils/extracts                 | strongest existing lead in the uploaded review                             |
| Lemon myrtle / *Backhousia citriodora* | Queensland-native, citral-rich, high-upside but needs bee-safety work      |
| Tea tree / *Melaleuca alternifolia*    | Australian comparator already discussed in varroa essential-oil literature |
| Local Myrtaceae and coastal plants     | only after the right people are invited into collection, cultural respect, stewardship and biodiscovery pathways |

Test sequence:

1. Beetle repellency/attraction assay.
2. Beetle mortality assay.
3. Adult bee safety assay.
4. Brood/queen behavioural screen.
5. Wax/honey taint and residue screen.
6. Slow-release trap material.
7. Small contained apiary pilot only if legal and safe.
8. Wider field trial only with permits and university/regulator sign-off.

The rule is: **no hive trial unless it passes bee-safety, residue and delivery gates.**

## Build-new-university pathway

This is possible, but the right sequence is not “announce a university tomorrow.” In Australia, “university” is a regulated status.

TEQSA says a new provider applying for first registration must apply for initial registration and initial course accreditation, must meet the Higher Education Standards Framework, and must include accreditation of at least one course with the registration application. ([TEQSA][16]) TEQSA also says Australian University status involves research that creates new knowledge and research training as a defining feature, with research quality benchmarked at world standard or national standing in Australian-specific fields. ([TEQSA][17])

So the practical pathway is:

### Stage 1: Minjerribah Bee Resilience Institute

Set up a not-for-profit institute first. It can run workshops, citizen science, school programs, beekeeper training, internships and partner university field schools without pretending to be a registered university.

Possible name:

**Minjerribah Institute for Bee Health, Biosecurity and Regenerative Futures**

It can partner with UQ, SCU, QUT, Griffith or TAFE Queensland to deliver accredited or microcredentialed training through already registered providers.

### Stage 2: University-backed field school

Create a formal Straddie field school:

**Bee Biosecurity, Island Ecology and Community Science Field School**

Students from UQ, SCU, QUT and Griffith come to Straddie. Local beekeepers teach practical hive reality. Researchers teach diagnostics and experimental design. Quandamooka partners shape Country, culture and governance content.

### Stage 3: Registered higher education provider

If the institute has governance, staff, policies, quality assurance, finances, student support and curriculum, it can prepare a TEQSA application as an independent higher education provider. That is where it could start offering its own accredited course.

First possible course:

**Graduate Certificate in Community Biosecurity and Pollinator Resilience**

### Stage 4: University College option

After credible teaching, research, finances and governance, the institute could apply to change category. TEQSA recognises provider categories including Institute of Higher Education, University College, Australian University and Overseas University, and registered providers can apply to change category. ([TEQSA][18])

### Stage 5: Australian University horizon

If strong research output, research training, multiple disciplines, academic governance and national/international standing become real, it could test whether full university status is appropriate.

The believable long-term identity is not just “bee university.” It could become:

**Minjerribah University of Regenerative Biosecurity and Human-Earth Systems**

Core schools:

| School                                                   | Focus                                                      |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------- |
| School of Bee Health and Pollinator Futures              | varroa, SHB, native bees, pollination                      |
| School of Island Biosecurity                             | invasive species, surveillance, community response         |
| School of Quandamooka Knowledge and Regenerative Futures | Country-led research, culture, governance                  |
| School of Bioactive Natural Products                     | native plant chemistry, benefit-sharing, safe formulations |
| School of XR, Data and Civic Science                     | digital twins, dashboards, AI-supported citizen science    |

That connects neatly with your broader Aura of Intelligence / G.A.J.R.A. Earth thinking: the lab becomes a real-world “small island, big system” prototype for joyful responsible abundance — not airy-fairy, but boots, bees, microscopes and community data. Bloody beautiful, honestly.
